act I

The brain

teaching software to play Connect 4

choosing the game

A game small enough to learn

Connect 4 has 7 columns, 6 rows and one decision per turn: which column. The rules fit on a napkin, but there are about 4.5 trillion legal positions. Nothing can memorise them all, so an AI has to learn to generalise.

Two simple opponents set the bar. Random drops a token in any legal column. Rules wins if it can, blocks if it must, builds threes and prefers the centre. Rules beats Random in about 99% of games.

reinforcement learning

Learning by playing

Nobody tells the agent what a good move is. It plays a game, gets a reward at the end (+10 for a win, −10 for a loss), and slowly works out which moves led there.

What it learns is a Q-value for every move: how much reward it expects if it plays that column now. Most of the time it plays the highest one. Sometimes it tries a random column, just to explore.

under the hood · Q-learning Q(s,a) ← Q(s,a) + α · [ r + γ · max Q(s′,a′) − Q(s,a) ] Nudge the old estimate toward "reward now plus the best value next". α is the learning rate, γ = 0.95 sets how much the future counts, and ε, the chance of a random move, decays from 1.0 to 0.05.

deep Q-network

From a lookup table to a neural network

My first agent kept a table: one score for every board it had seen. After training it held 187,000 entries and still knew nothing about a board it had never met.

A Deep Q-Network swaps the table for a neural network. The board goes in, seven scores come out, and similar boards get similar scores, so what it learns carries over.

Both agents learned to beat random play, because that is what they trained against. An agent only gets as good as its sparring partners.

convolutional network

A network that sees lines

Four in a row is a visual pattern, so the next network looks at the board the way you do. A convolutional network slides small filters across the grid and lights up when it finds lines, threats and blocks.

what went wrong in training

Training fought back

Getting from "plays" to "plays well" took most of the time. Four problems kept coming back.

exploding scores

The numbers ran away

Each estimate fed the next until Q-values blew up. Fixed with a 0.95 discount, clamping to ±100 and clipping the gradients.

forgetting

New opponent, old skills gone

Switching sparring partners erased what it knew. Fixed with a curriculum that overlaps phases by 4,000 games and a memory that keeps old games.

fake self-play

Playing a stale copy of itself

Its "self" was a snapshot from 500 games ago. Fixed with a league of 5 past versions: win 60% of games to move up a tier.

too little feedback

Win or lose says too little

One reward per game hides which move mattered. Fixed with small signals: +2 for a block, −3 for a missed block, −5 for a missed win.

the tournament

A team beats any single player

After more than a million training games, I put the saved versions in a round robin: 3,500 games each. The winner was a team: the top four networks voting on every move.

On screen, the AI was now a real opponent. But it lived on a screen, and I wanted it across the table.

act II

The eyes

giving the brain a body, and a way to see

from software to hardware

Give the brain a body

The body is an SO-101, an open-source robot arm with six servo motors that I drive from Python with Hugging Face's lerobot library.

It has no eyes, so a small USB camera sits on the wrist. Because the camera moves with the hand, it always sees where the hand is about to go.

The board lies flat on the table instead of standing upright. A flat grid is also what checkers and chess look like, so the same setup can grow beyond Connect 4. A move means: go to the token supply, grab a token, carry it, drop it in the chosen cell.

computer vision

How the robot reads the board

Before the AI can think, the camera has to turn a photo into a grid of numbers. It does that in four steps.

under the hood · HSV colourHue, saturation, value: colour is kept apart from brightness, so a shadow changes V but barely changes H. Green is hue 35–90 and purple 125–165 on OpenCV's scale. The colour filters still loved daylight and hated lamps.
a sanity check from physicsTokens can't float. A "token" with an empty cell below it must be glare on the board, so it gets removed before the AI ever sees it.

where things are

From pixels to millimetres

"Purple in D3" is enough for the AI. The arm needs to know where D3 is on the table, in millimetres.

A flat table seen at an angle is a stretched rectangle, and a homography undoes the stretch. Four known points are enough to compute it. They come from ArUco markers: printed squares the camera finds and identifies on its own.

One more fix: the wide-angle lens bends straight lines, up to about 7 mm off. A one-time lens calibration straightens the image first.

under the hood[u v 1]ᵀ ∝ H · [x y 1]ᵀH is a 3 × 3 matrix with 8 unknowns. Each point pair gives two equations, so four points solve it; the 16 marker corners make it robust.

act III

The arm

from "that square" to joint angles

the first approach

First attempt: teach every cell

My first approach was hands-on. I moved the arm over each cell and saved its joint angles: 40 poses in total. For a spot in between, the software blended the 8 nearest saved poses.

It worked near the taught points and broke everywhere else. Moving the board 5 mm made every pose wrong. Averaging joint angles is not the same as averaging positions, so the blends drifted. And a new game would mean teaching every square again: 64 for chess.

the key idea

Forward and inverse kinematics

Forward kinematics answers an easy question: given every joint angle, where is the fingertip? Chain the links together: turn, move along the link, turn again. There is exactly one answer.

Inverse kinematics asks the reverse: given where the fingertip must go, which angles get it there? There may be several answers, or none.

Think of reaching for a cup. You don't plan "shoulder 30°, elbow 70°". You look at the cup and your arm works it out. IK gives the robot that skill.

how the solver works

Solving it: guess, check, adjust

  1. Guess a set of angles. The solver starts from three typical reaching shapes.
  2. Check with forward kinematics how far the fingertip lands from the target.
  3. Adjust the angles in whichever direction shrinks that distance, and repeat until the error is under 1 mm.

It also has a preference: keep the gripper pointing straight down, and lean outward in 10° steps, up to 50°, only when a cell is too far to reach vertically.

the payoff

Why this generalises

Seen from above, a board game is just a grid of points on a table. IK can reach any point within the arm's range, so every game turns into a list of coordinates.

calibration

Making the maths match the metal

IK is only as good as its model of the arm, and a real arm never matches the drawing exactly. Joints sit a few degrees off, and the base is not quite where you think.

So I printed a calibration mat with 15 crosshairs to touch and 4 ArUco markers for the camera. Then I fitted 10 numbers to the touches: joint offsets, the base's position and rotation, and the fingertip offset.

2.3 mm is well inside a cell: good enough to drop a token where it belongs.

the detours

What I tripped over

Most of these looked like an arm problem. Most were measurement problems.

a LiDAR on the wrist?

Not needed

±6 cm accuracy and a 20 cm blind zone. The table is flat, so the height is already known.

two unit systems

Degrees vs −100…100

Scripts spoke different units and loaded different calibration files, up to ~9° apart.

the printer

100 mm became 97.5

The mat printed slightly small. One scale factor fixed it.

lazy servos

They stop ~1.5° short

Under load. Now the arm reads its encoders, nudges, and repeats until they match.

the far row

Taught at a tilt

Taught leaning, used vertical: the model guessed 5 mm too low. Teach the pose you will use.

the lens

Fisheye bends lines

Predicted crosses drifted ~7 mm. A one-time lens calibration straightens the image.

epilogue

Putting it together

one move, from photo to token

One move: see, think, act

Every game, the camera finds the board, reads the tokens and works out where each cell sits on the table. The arm and the camera are calibrated against the table once. After that, the board can sit anywhere the arm reaches.
