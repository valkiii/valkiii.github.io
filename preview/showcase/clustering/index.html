Cluster the Reasons

Supervised clustering · a worked example on simulated customers

Cluster the reasons.

Supervised clustering lets a good-enough model tell the clustering what matters. Here it turns a gut-feeling campaign rule into investor personas for a made-up money app, and the method travels well beyond finance.

scroll slowly

The request

Among our customers, who should get a Pocket campaign, and who should get a Managed one?

Meridian, a made-up money app, offers two ways to invest: Pocket, a self-directed trading app, and Managed, portfolios run with an adviser. Its marketing team wants better campaigns than one age rule. Every customer on this page is simulated.

The rule on the table

Aged 25 to 50 → Pocket.
Everyone else → Managed.

one variable, and it covers most investors…

The approach

Cluster the model's explanations, not the customers

We proposed data-driven personas instead. The obvious tool is clustering, and on raw customer data it fails. The fix is one extra step.

the obvious move

Cluster the raw customer data

Reduce everything to two dimensions and look for groups. That step is sound: in high dimensions, density estimates degrade and HDBSCAN slows down, and the literature shows that reducing with UMAP first considerably improves clustering. But here you get one overlapping cloud. On customer data, the clumps follow spending and household, and the biggest or noisiest columns dominate. Clustering doesn't know what we're asking.

the extra step

Ask a model first

A simple model predicts who holds Pocket and who holds Managed. It only needs to be good enough to learn which features matter. SHAP then says, for every customer and feature, how far that feature pushes them towards one product or the other.

supervised clustering

Cluster the explanations

Every feature is now in the same unit, and features that don't matter shrink to zero. Customers group by why they hold a product, and the groups can be named.

Step one · the good-enough model

Gradient boosting: a hundred small trees, adding up

The model's job is to learn which features separate Pocket holders from Managed holders, not to be perfect.

gradient boosting

Each tree asks a few questions

Gradient boosting builds small trees one after another (this simulation uses XGBoost; LightGBM works the same way and SHAP explains it the same way). Each new tree corrects the mistakes of the ones before it, and their answers add up to a score: the log-odds of holding Pocket. These are the first three trees it learned: app logins, investment clicks and advisor contacts come up straight away.

good enough, not perfect

Clearly better than chance

On customers it never saw, it ranks a Pocket holder above a Managed holder 82% of the time. As in real life, one in seven customers holds the product their profile doesn't predict. We don't use its predictions: it's a lens on which features matter, and for that it only has to clearly beat chance.

Step two · SHAP values

Why the model says what it says, customer by customer

SHAP splits each customer's score into one contribution per feature. Starting from the average customer, the contributions add up exactly to the score.

customer A, 38

99% Pocket, because of the app

Start from the average customer. Fifteen app logins a month push the score up the most; few advisor contacts and small savings add more. Every bar is in the same unit, so a count of logins and a balance in euros can be compared directly.

customer B, 70

99% Pocket, for a different reason

The same score, but here age does the work and app logins barely matter. The score alone would put A and B together; SHAP keeps them apart. And B falls outside the age rule.

every customer

The matrix we cluster

One row of SHAP values per customer, each from a model that never saw that customer (out-of-fold). The five features that drive the choice spread out; card spend, payments and the other unrelated columns sit at zero, so they drop out of the distance. This matrix, not the raw data, goes into the clustering.

Five reasons, five personas

Three reasons to hold Pocket, two to hold Managed. The split between products is by design; the reasons inside each side are the finding.

Colour the map by one raw feature at a time. Investment-page clicks light up the self-directed traders and no one else.

Who's older

Age marks out one Pocket persona: the digitally active 55+. The age rule would send all of them the Managed campaign.

No pattern, on purpose

Spending has nothing to do with the product choice, so the model gave it no weight. It's the kind of column that drowns plain clustering.

What it changes

The right product, the right message, a fair test

Targeting

A short rule per persona replaces the age range. Fewer customers are selected, each with a much clearer product lean.

Content

The persona says why, so each persona gets its own message: the Pocket offer for app-first starters and traders, an in-app nudge for the digitally active 55+, and Managed through an adviser for the rest.

Existing customers

Customers whose persona fits the other product better get a suggestion to switch.

next: prove it

Personas are a hypothesis until a test says otherwise. The next step is a randomised test of the persona rules against the age rule and a no-contact holdout, with the success metric fixed in advance.

A small change in method turns a vague request into personas a marketing team can use within a week.

It isn't specific to finance either. Wherever a model can explain an outcome (why subscribers cancel, why machines fail, why students drop out), you can cluster the explanations.

Data: a published simulation (1,000 customers; 50 columns, 5 of which matter), with label noise added so that one in seven customers holds the "other" product, and the columns relabelled as features of a money app. Meridian, Pocket and Managed are made up. Model: XGBoost, with SHAP computed out of fold. Method, diagram and simulation adapted from "Supervised Clustering for Subgroup Discovery" (ECML PKDD 2021). UMAP before clustering: Allaoui, Kherfi and Cheriet (ICISP 2020).
